Ask a property management owner what keeps them up at night and you will hear about staffing long before you hear about fees. Ask which role is hardest to fill and the answer is almost always the same one: a good maintenance technician.

The reason is worth stating plainly. Maintenance is the part of this business that residents and owners actually feel. A board renews because the building works. A resident renews because the heat came back on a Saturday. Everything else your firm does well gets undone by a maintenance operation that cannot keep up.

## Why the hiring is genuinely hard

The shortage is real and it is not unique to property management. Skilled trades across the country have an aging workforce and fewer people entering than leaving. In New Jersey, management firms compete for the same technicians as mechanical contractors, facilities departments, and large apartment operators, and several of those can pay more.

There is also a quieter problem. Many management firms recruit only when someone resigns, which means every search starts under pressure and ends with whoever is available. Hiring under pressure is how a firm ends up with a technician who does not work out, which creates another vacancy six months later.

## Widen what you will accept, then train

The firms that staff well have usually made the same decision: they stopped looking exclusively for someone who already does the job.

General maintenance work does not require a trade license, and plenty of excellent technicians learned entirely on the job. Someone with real mechanical ability from an unrelated field, a strong helper from a trades company, or a graduate of a county vocational program can become a capable technician within a year if somebody teaches them.

That means you need somebody who can teach. The firms that train well pair a new technician with an experienced one for long enough to matter, give them a defined list of what they should be able to handle unsupervised by month three and month six, and check in against it. The ones that struggle hand a new hire a van and a work order queue on day two.

## Pay for the license, and say so in the posting

Plumbing, electrical, and heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) work requires the licensing that the job and the state call for. Those credentials cost money and time, and a technician working toward one is making a real investment.

Covering that cost is one of the least expensive retention tools available, and it is strikingly effective. A firm that pays for exam fees, continuing education, and the hours spent in class is a firm that a technician has a reason to stay with. Put it in the job posting rather than saving it for the offer, because it is the sentence that gets the application.

The same applies to certifications that carry across employers, including Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Section 608 refrigerant certification for anyone touching air conditioning equipment.

## Fix the on-call rotation before you lose someone to it

The most common reason a good technician leaves a management firm has nothing to do with pay. It is the on-call phone.

In a small firm, after-hours coverage often lands on one or two people permanently, which turns every weekend into a maybe. That arrangement works until the technician's family stops tolerating it, and then it ends abruptly.

Three changes help. Rotate the phone across enough people that any one person carries it predictably rather than constantly. Pay for the rotation itself rather than only for the calls that come in, because the burden is the availability. And triage properly, so that a resident reporting a dripping faucet at eleven at night produces a next-morning work order rather than a truck roll.

## Give the work structure

Technicians consistently tell us the same things make a job better, and almost none of them are expensive.

A stocked truck and a parts account save a technician the daily indignity of buying a part at retail and expensing it. A work order system that holds the history means they arrive knowing what happened last time. A schedule they can plan around matters more than most owners expect, because the alternative is a day of interruptions that never ends.

The firms that get this right tend to lose fewer people to the mechanical contractor down the road, even when that contractor pays slightly more.

## Hire before you need to

The hardest change to make, and the one that matters most, is hiring ahead of the work rather than behind it.

Most owners cannot do this easily, because an extra technician is a real cost carried against uncertain future work, and the money comes out of the owner's own pocket. That is the honest reason so many firms run short-staffed.

It is also one of the clearest advantages of having a parent company fund the hire. At Orchard Property Management we add maintenance capacity ahead of the work and recruit continuously across our companies rather than in a scramble, which means a vacancy is a reassignment rather than a crisis.

## Where this connects to everything else

A management firm with a strong maintenance operation retains clients, renews agreements, and wins referrals from boards who have watched it handle an emergency well. A firm with a weak one loses contracts for reasons that show up in a board meeting long before they show up in the financial statements.
