Owners usually open this conversation with a revenue figure, and we understand why. Revenue is the number you track, the number your accountant reports, and the number another owner quoted at a conference. It is also the number that tells a buyer the least.

What a serious buyer wants to know is how much of that revenue will still be there in three years, and how much of it depends on you personally. Everything below follows from those two questions.

## Recurring management fees carry the most weight

The healthiest firms we look at earn the large majority of their revenue from management agreements that renew quietly, year after year. That income is predictable, it survives a change of ownership, and it reflects a service standard that clients have already decided they want to keep.

Ancillary income is useful, and it is weighted differently. Lease renewal fees, maintenance markups, transfer and resale certificate fees, and project management fees all add profit, and some of them add a great deal of it. They also move with the market, and a buyer discounts them accordingly.

Brokerage and development income usually gets discounted the hardest. There is nothing wrong with running those lines, and plenty of excellent firms do. A buyer simply treats them as a different business with a different risk profile, and values them separately.

## Client tenure tells the real story

Two firms can report the same management fee revenue and be worth very different amounts. The difference usually sits in how long clients have stayed.

We look closely at the age of each management agreement, the churn rate over the past three to five years, and why departures happened. A community that left because it sold to a developer reads very differently from a community that left because meetings ran badly for a year. We also look at concentration. Where one client or one association represents a large share of revenue, that single relationship becomes the most important item in the whole review.

The firms that score best here are rarely the cheapest in their market. They are the ones that answer the phone.

## Your team is part of the asset

In property management, institutional memory lives in people. A community association manager who knows the history of a building, which vendor did the roof, and how the board voted on it saves a community money and argument every single year. A maintenance technician who has serviced the same portfolio for a decade diagnoses in ten minutes what a new hire takes a day to find.

So we look at tenure, at how many portfolios each manager carries, at whether the accounting function depends on one person, and at what happens if that person is out for a month. A firm where the owner is also the controller, the business developer, and the escalation point for every unhappy board is a firm with a single point of failure, and a buyer prices that.

The good news is that this is the most improvable item on the list.

## Clean books are worth real money

Nobody expects an owner-run firm to produce audited statements. We do expect the numbers to reconcile, and we expect to be able to trace revenue back to the agreements that produce it.

Three things make the biggest difference. Keep personal expenses out of the company, or at least list them clearly so they can be added back without a debate. Make sure trust and operating accounts are properly separated and reconciled, which matters more in this industry than almost any other. And keep a current schedule of management agreements showing the client, the start date, the fee structure, the renewal terms, and whether assignment requires consent.

An owner who hands over that schedule in the first week shortens diligence by a month and raises a buyer's confidence in everything else.

## Software and process reduce risk

A buyer is trying to work out how much has to change after closing. A firm running a modern property management platform, with owner and resident portals in active use and work orders flowing through a system rather than a group text, needs far less investment and carries far less risk.

This is not about having the newest tool. We have bought firms running software we would not have chosen and left it alone for a year, because changing a platform during a transition creates risk for the people we just promised continuity to. It is about whether the process exists somewhere other than in the owner's head.

## What you can improve before you ever take a call

Owners sometimes ask what to do if a sale is two or three years away. The answer is encouraging, because most of the levers are operational rather than financial.

Build a second layer of leadership, and let that person handle escalations and client relationships so the firm is not synonymous with you. Document the management agreements and keep the schedule current. Separate your personal finances from the business. Reduce concentration by growing the smaller clients rather than chasing one large one. And invest in the accounting function, which is the single most common gap we find and the most expensive one to fix after closing.

Owners who start two or three years ahead almost always end up with a cleaner process and a better outcome. That is why we are glad to have the first conversation long before anyone is ready.
