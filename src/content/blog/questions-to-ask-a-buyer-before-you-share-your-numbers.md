Most owners come into a first call expecting to be interviewed. A buyer asks about revenue, doors under management, staff count, and client tenure, and an hour later the owner has given away a clear picture of the business while learning almost nothing in return.

You are allowed to run that call differently. Nothing below requires a non-disclosure agreement, an accountant, or any financial disclosure at all, and the answers tell you a great deal about who you are dealing with.

## Who actually owns you after closing?

Start here, because everything else follows from it. Ask who the capital behind the buyer belongs to, whether there is a fund with a defined life, and what the expected holding period is.

A buyer backed by a fund with a seven-year life has to sell your company again inside that window. That is not dishonest, and it is not necessarily bad for you at closing. It does mean that decisions in years two through five will be shaped by preparing for a resale, and it means your staff will go through this a second time.

A buyer who holds indefinitely has no such clock. Ask directly which one you are talking to, and ask them to say it plainly.

## How many of these have you done, and may I call one?

Experience in this specific situation matters more than deal volume. Ask how many founder-owned companies the buyer has partnered with, how many were in property management or an adjacent service business, and what happened to the employees in each.

Then ask for a reference, and make it a specific one. Ask to speak with a seller from two or three years ago rather than one from last quarter. The seller who closed recently is still in the honeymoon period. The seller from three years ago knows whether the promises held.

A buyer who will not give you a reference after a serious conversation is telling you something.

## What happens to my staff in the first ninety days?

Ask for specifics rather than reassurance. Which roles are retained. Whether anyone is designated as redundant at closing. Whether managers keep their portfolios or get reshuffled. Who the team reports to on the Monday after. What happens to pay, benefits, and paid time off.

In property management, the follow-up question matters just as much. Ask whether community association managers stay with their communities. A buyer who plans to reorganize portfolios by geography or by size is planning to break the relationships that make your firm valuable, and you should know that before you sell rather than after.

## What happens to the name?

A local name in this industry carries years of referrals from boards, owners, attorneys, brokers, and other managers. Ask whether the buyer intends to keep it, rebrand it immediately, or phase it over time.

There is no single correct answer. Some owners want the name retired, and some would find a rebrand genuinely painful. What matters is that the buyer tells you now, in the first conversation, rather than letting you find out when the new signage arrives.

## How will you handle client consents and board communication?

This is the question that separates buyers who understand property management from buyers who understand acquisitions generally.

Many management agreements require consent before they can be assigned. Community association contracts often require a board vote. Ask the buyer how they have handled that before, when they would want to approach boards, and who would be in the room. A buyer who has not thought about this will improvise, and improvising in front of a board meeting is how a smooth transition becomes a messy one.

## What stays confidential, and for how long?

Ask who inside the buyer's organization sees your information, whether the non-disclosure agreement is mutual, and what happens to your materials if the conversation ends.

Then ask the practical version of the same question. Will anyone contact your employees, clients, board members, or vendors without your written permission? The answer should be no, without qualification, until you say otherwise.

## What does your diligence actually involve?

Ask what documents the buyer needs, in what order, and how long each stage takes. Ask when they would want to visit, whether that visit can happen outside business hours, and how they would explain their presence to your staff if someone asks.

A buyer with a defined process will answer quickly and specifically. A buyer who answers vaguely has either not done this often or does not intend to tell you.

## What would you change in year one?

This question tends to produce the most revealing answer of the call, because it is hard to prepare for.

Listen for whether the changes are investments or extractions. Hiring a second accountant, replacing a platform the team hates, funding a service vehicle, and adding after-hours coverage are investments. Raising management fees across the portfolio, cutting the maintenance team, and consolidating your office into another city are extractions. Both are legitimate business strategies. Only one of them is the one you probably want for the people you are leaving behind.

## Can I stay involved, and can I leave?

Ask about both, because the answer should be yours to choose. Some owners want to retire at closing. Others want a defined transition period. A few want to keep running the firm for years because they enjoy it.

Be careful with any buyer who requires a long employment commitment as a condition of the price. That arrangement can work well, and it can also turn into years of working for someone else inside the company you used to own.

## A note on brokers

You do not need a broker or an investment banker to have these conversations, and plenty of owners prefer to have one. Either way, keep your own attorney and accountant involved before you sign anything, including the letter of intent. A letter of intent is usually non-binding on price and very much binding on exclusivity, and that distinction has cost owners real money.
